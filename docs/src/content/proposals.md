# Proposals

A proposal is work that has been asked for and not yet sold. It is the one record two audiences
share: the people who sell it and the people who would do it.

## The flow

```text
draft → evaluating → evaluated → documents-review → sent → accepted → converted
                                                         ↘ declined
```

| Status | Whose move |
| --- | --- |
| **Draft** | Sales writes down what was asked for |
| **Being evaluated** | With the delivery side for an effort figure |
| **Effort agreed** | Back with sales |
| **Documents in review** | A manager checks the paperwork |
| **Offer sent** | With the client |
| **Accepted** / **Declined** | The client's answer, with a reason |
| **Engagement created** | It became real work |

Each side only sees the buttons that are theirs, and a button that cannot be pressed yet says why
— *"the effort has not been agreed yet, so there is nothing firm to put in the paperwork"*.

## The two effort figures

Sales records what they think it is. The delivery side records what it actually takes. **Both are
kept**, and shown side by side, because the disagreement is the useful part: *five days sold, nine
days needed* is a fact somebody should see every time.

The days that go on the engagement when it converts are the agreed ones.

> [!tip]
> When somebody types an estimate, the app shows what jobs of that type have actually taken —
> from the time logged against past engagements, not from what they were sold for.

## Paperwork

Generate the NDA, the permission to attack, the offer or the statement of work from your own
templates — see [Templates](/templates). Regenerating replaces the previous file of that type
rather than adding a second.

Before an offer can be sent:

- the paperwork must have been **generated by this app**, not uploaded as somebody's hand-edited
  copy,
- and **signed off** by a manager. Signing a client's contract as fit to leave the building is an
  authority, not a skill, so it is deliberately narrower than "does the work".

## Why it was won or lost

Closing a proposal asks for a reason. A loss **must** give one — a reason nobody is required to
give is a field that is empty nine times in ten and worth nothing. A win is asked the same
question, because why we win is the half nobody writes down.

Losing to a competitor asks who to, and the names are counted.

The **Why we win and lose** card turns six months of that into something a rate card or a hiring
plan can answer, along with how long a decision typically takes — measured from the offer going
out, not from when the enquiry arrived.

## Worth another call

A proposal lost on **timing**, **budget** or **silence** was never a no; it was a not-now, and the
not-now expires. Six months later that is the warmest list a sales section has.

The card lists them, leaves out anybody already back in the pipeline, and its button raises this
year's proposal from last year's.

## Cloning

**Clone for next time** copies the scope, the constraints and the contacts, moves the dates a year
on and takes a new reference.

What it deliberately does *not* copy: the agreed estimate — that belonged to the job as it was
scoped then — and the paperwork, because an NDA is signed and a permission to attack names dates.
A clone is a draft of the *request*, and goes round the loop again.

## Discussion

Comments live on the proposal, open to both audiences. It is the one place the two sides are meant
to disagree in writing: *"nine days for 40 hosts — is the AD estate inside that figure?"*

Every other panel belongs to one side or the other. This one does not.

## The kickoff

Recorded by whoever was on the call, either side. It holds the date, who attended, the emergency
contact and what was agreed — which is what a permission to attack is written from, and what
somebody actually reaches for at two in the morning when a test takes something down.

## Becoming an engagement

**Create the engagement** — the work side's action, not sales' — carries across the client, the
contacts, the type, the reference and the days sold. If the proposal was a retainer, the engagement
is set up to nudge when the next one is due rather than creating them all at once.
